# What to Leave for a Cat Sitter: The Complete Checklist

Cats don't need walking, so it's tempting to think a cat sitter needs less handover than a dog sitter does. In some ways that's true. In the ways that matter — routine, hiding, medication, litter — cats are just as particular, they just show it differently.

## The short answer

Cover feeding and litter specifics, where they'll likely hide, medical and emergency authorisation, and what's actually normal versus what's worth a call. Most cat-sitting problems aren't emergencies — they're a sitter panicking over completely normal cat behaviour because nobody mentioned it in advance.

## 1. Feeding

- What food, how much, and how often — cats can be surprisingly particular about both brand and bowl
- Any feeding quirks (some cats need a specific spot, some need food refreshed rather than topped up)
- Fresh water — where the bowl or fountain lives, and whether it needs cleaning
- Treats — how many, and any that are off-limits

## 2. Litter

This is the one people underexplain. Leave specifics: type of litter (cats can refuse to use a box with unfamiliar litter), how often it needs scooping, and where spare litter and bags are kept. A cat that stops using the box is usually telling you something — stress, a dirty box, or occasionally a medical issue — so it's worth flagging as something to mention, not ignore.

## 3. Where they'll probably hide

Plenty of cats disappear when a stranger arrives and don't reappear for hours. That's normal, not an emergency — but only if the sitter's been told to expect it. Mention your cat's usual hiding spots and roughly how long they take to warm up, so a sitter doesn't spend day one convinced something's wrong.

## 4. Medical and emergency

- Your vet's name, clinic, and phone number
- Medication — what, how much, when, and how you actually get it into a cat that has opinions about that
- Written authorisation for emergency treatment, plus a spending limit — sitters who aren't the owner are often asked for exactly this at the clinic counter
- A backup contact if you can't be reached

## 5. Indoor safety

- Whether your cat is strictly indoor and what that means for the sitter (windows, doors, screens)
- Any known escape attempts or risk areas
- What to do if your cat does get out

## 6. House logistics

- Spare key location, and who else has one
- Alarm code, if there is one
- Any rooms or areas that are off-limits

## The part that's easy to leave out

Most of this only matters once — right before you leave — and by the next sit, half of it's changed. New litter brand, new medication dose, a vet you've since switched to. A note from six months ago is worse than no note, because it reads as current when it isn't.
