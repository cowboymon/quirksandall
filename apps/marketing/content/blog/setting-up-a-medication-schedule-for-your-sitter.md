# Setting Up a Medication Schedule Your Sitter Can Actually Follow

"She gets a tablet twice a day" sounds like a complete instruction until someone who isn't you actually has to carry it out. Twice a day when, exactly? In food or by hand? What if she spits it out — try again, or leave it? A medication instruction that's clear in your head is often missing half of what a sitter actually needs, not because you left it out on purpose, but because you've never had to explain it to anyone before.

## The short answer

A medication schedule that actually works has four things for every single medication: the exact dose, the exact timing, the specific method that works for your pet, and what to do if it doesn't go to plan. Missing any one of the four turns a simple task into a guessing game, usually at the exact moment your sitter is least equipped to guess.

## Dose and timing — more specific than you think

"Twice a day" and "one in the morning" both sound precise until they're not. What actually removes ambiguity:

- The exact times, not just "morning and night" — a four-hour gap either side of a stated time matters for some medications and not others, and your sitter has no way to know which without being told
- Whether it's given with food, on an empty stomach, or it doesn't matter — some medications need to be taken with food and others need an empty stomach, and getting this backwards can affect how well the medication works
- What happens if a dose is missed or given late — whether to give it as soon as they remember, skip it, or call you, since this varies by medication and isn't something a sitter should be guessing at under pressure

## The method that actually works for your pet

This is the detail that gets left out most often, because it feels less like information and more like just "how you do it." But it's often the single hardest part for a new sitter to figure out alone.

Common approaches, and why the specific one matters:

- **Hidden in a small amount of food** — works well for many pets, but some medications shouldn't be crushed or mixed with food since it can affect how they're absorbed, so this needs your vet's confirmation, not just a guess
- **Pill pockets or a treat wrap** — often the easiest method for a food-motivated pet, though some pets get wise to it over time and need the treat varied
- **The "chaser" technique** — a plain treat first, then the one with the medication hidden inside, then another plain treat, so your pet doesn't start associating every treat with medication
- **By hand, directly** — for pets who see through every disguise, worth describing exactly how you do it, since a first attempt from someone unfamiliar with the technique is often the one that goes wrong

Whichever method is yours, write down the specific version — not "hide it in food" but "hide it in a teaspoon of wet food, offered on its own before the rest of the meal." That level of detail is what turns a stressful ten minutes into a routine one.

## What to do when it doesn't go to plan

Every pet eventually spits one out, refuses a treat, or fights a dose. A sitter without a plan for this moment either gives up and calls you in a panic, or improvises something that might not be safe. Worth covering in advance:

- Whether it's fine to try again with a different method, or whether that particular medication shouldn't be re-dosed
- At what point a missed or refused dose is worth a call to you versus just noting it and moving on
- Whether your pet has ever had a reaction worth watching for after a dose

## Why this deserves its own document, not a line in a bigger one

Feeding times and walk schedules can survive being one line each in a longer handover. Medication can't — it's the one category where an unclear instruction has a real, physical consequence, not just an inconvenience.
