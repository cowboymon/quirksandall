# Briefing a Sitter on a Senior Dog

A young dog's handover is mostly about routine and personality. A senior dog's handover needs a third category entirely: what's normal ageing, what's manageable discomfort, and what's worth an urgent call — because senior dogs rarely show pain in obvious ways, and a sitter without that context can easily miss it, or panic over something perfectly ordinary.

## The short answer

Senior dogs communicate discomfort through small, easy-to-miss changes rather than obvious limping or crying, so the most useful thing you can hand over isn't a list of conditions — it's what's already normal for your dog specifically, so a sitter can tell the difference between "how he always is" and "something's changed."

## Mobility: the subtle signs

Limping is a late-stage sign, not an early one. What actually shows up first:

- Hesitating before jumping onto the couch, into the car, or onto the bed
- Stiffness after resting that eases once they're up and moving
- Slower to rise, or taking longer on stairs
- A shorter, slower version of their normal walk, without obvious limping

If any of this is already true for your dog, say so plainly — a sitter who doesn't know "he's always a bit stiff getting up in the morning" may mistake a completely normal moment for a new problem, or the reverse: mistake a genuinely new issue for business as usual because nobody flagged the baseline.

## Medication and mobility aids

- **Joint medication or supplements** — what, dose, and timing, using the same specificity as any other medication instruction
- **Physical aids in use** — ramps instead of stairs, non-slip mats or rugs on slippery floors, an orthopedic bed — and why they matter, so a sitter doesn't remove or skip them assuming they're optional
- **Any temperature sensitivity** — some senior dogs feel joint stiffness more in cold weather, which can change how much walking or warm-up time they need before heading out

## Behaviour changes that are actually pain, not just "getting old"

Senior dogs often express discomfort through behaviour rather than obvious physical signs:

- Increased irritability, or snapping when previously tolerant of handling
- Withdrawal — less interest in greeting people, playing, or engaging
- Changes in sleep, including more restlessness or waking more often
- Persistent licking or chewing at a particular joint or spot

None of this is "just aging" to write off without mention — but it's also not necessarily an emergency. It's context a sitter needs to interpret correctly rather than either ignoring or over-reacting to.

## What's manageable versus what's worth a call

**Generally expected, with the accommodations above:**
- Slower walks, more rest breaks, reluctance to jump without help
- Stiffness after lying down that eases with movement

**Worth a call to you or the vet:**
- A sudden change from their normal baseline — new limping, new reluctance to move at all, or a new refusal to eat
- Crying out, or any sign of acute pain rather than general stiffness
- Being unable to stand or walk without help, or repeated falls
- Any behaviour that seems like a sudden decline rather than the gradual pattern you're used to

## What to actually hand over

- What's already normal for your dog — their existing baseline for stiffness, pace, and stair tolerance
- Joint medication or supplements, with the same dose/timing detail as any other medication
- Mobility aids in use, and why they matter
- The specific point at which a change is worth a call, since "seems a bit stiffer" means very little without a baseline to compare it to

## Why this matters more with a senior dog, not less

It's tempting to think a senior dog needs a lighter handover — less energy, fewer walks, a simpler routine. In practice they need a more detailed one, because the margin for a sitter misreading a subtle sign is exactly where a real problem gets missed for days.
