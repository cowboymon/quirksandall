# The Post-Trip Debrief: What to Ask Your Sitter When You Get Home

Most of what's written about pet sitting stops the moment you walk back in the door. You get the "how was she?" text, a photo of your dog looking pleased with themselves, maybe a note about the vet bill if something happened. Then everyone moves on. That's the point where almost everyone stops — and it's also the exact point where the most useful information of the whole stay goes to waste.

## The short answer

Your sitter just spent days learning things about your pet that you don't currently know. Not the big stuff — you'd have heard about that already. The small stuff: which command actually landed, what routine held up, what they've quietly started doing differently. A five-minute debrief when you're back turns that into information for the next stay, instead of letting it evaporate.

## Why a sitter notices things you don't

It's not that you're not paying attention to your own pet. It's that you're paying attention to them every single day, which means slow changes are invisible to you in a way they're not to someone meeting your pet fresh. You don't notice your dog's gone a bit stiffer on stairs when you watch it happen gradually over months. You don't notice you've started saying "down" three different ways until someone new tries it once, literally, and it doesn't land. A sitter walks in with no history and no assumptions — so the things that have quietly shifted, or that you've unconsciously worked around, are often the first things they pick up on.

That's what makes a sitter's observations different from just "extra information." You already know your pet. What you're missing is the outside view — what someone would notice about them without three years of familiarity smoothing it over. That's genuinely hard to get any other way.

## What to actually ask

**Commands and responsiveness**
- Which commands worked reliably, and which ones didn't land at all
- Did they find a version or tone that worked better than what you'd written down
- Any new command they ended up using that you hadn't mentioned

**Feeding and routine**
- Did your pet eat normally, or was there a change worth noting
- Any adjustment the sitter made that actually worked better than your usual routine
- Did sleep, toileting or energy levels look different to what you'd described

**Behaviour and quirks**
- Anything that matched what you'd written, and anything that didn't
- A trigger or reaction the sitter saw that wasn't on your list
- Whether a known quirk showed up as described, or looked different this time

**Anything that's changed**
- Has anything shifted enough that it's worth updating before the next sitter — a new habit, a food preference, a command that no longer works the way it used to

## Why this is more than a nice-to-have

Pets change. A command that worked reliably six months ago might not anymore. A food they used to eat without complaint might get quietly refused. None of that shows up as a single dramatic moment — it shows up as a gradual drift that only someone without your day-to-day familiarity would clock in a few days flat.

## Closing the loop

The debrief is only useful if it actually changes something. If your sitter mentions your dog now responds better to "off" than "down," or that your cat's stopped using the fountain, that's not a fun fact — it's an update your next handover needs. Otherwise you've collected genuinely useful information and then filed it nowhere, which means the next sitter starts from the same outdated instructions the last one did.
