# What to Do When Your Pet Actually Goes Missing

Every prevention guide ends with a version of the same line: "if it happens anyway, stay calm and contact the owner." True, but not exactly a plan. If you're reading this because it's already happened — to you, or to a sitter looking after your pet right now — here's what actually helps, in order.

## The short answer

Confirm they're genuinely missing rather than just hiding, act in the first few hours while the search radius is still small, use the right combination of physical searching and online alerts, and know when to widen the search versus when to wait. Speed and method matter more than panic ever will.

## First: are they actually missing?

For cats especially, "missing" and "hiding" get confused constantly. Before you treat this as a search-and-recovery situation:

- Check every room, including ones your pet doesn't usually have access to — an open cupboard or a gap behind furniture is a common false alarm
- For cats, check up (wardrobes, high shelves) and down (under beds, inside boxes) — a startled cat goes as far from the source of the fright as the room allows
- Confirm a door or window really was open long enough for an exit, rather than assuming based on a moment of panic

If you've done a proper search of the property and there's still no sign of them after a reasonable window, move to an active search.

## The first hour matters most

Most escaped pets — especially cats — don't go far initially. They tend to hide close to home rather than run, which is exactly why the first search should be slow and close-range, not a wide sweep of the neighbourhood.

- **Search on foot, at their level.** Crouch down and check under cars, decks, sheds and dense garden beds within a few houses in each direction, not just your own yard.
- **Bring something familiar.** A favourite toy, their carrier, or a container of food — cats in particular often won't come to a call from a stranger but will respond to something that smells like home.
- **Search at dawn or dusk if the escape happened during the day.** Many pets, cats especially, stay hidden during daylight and become more active — and more findable — as things quiet down.
- **Check with immediate neighbours directly**, not just online. A knock on the door often surfaces a sighting that never makes it to a lost-pet group.

## Widening the search

If the first few hours don't turn anything up:

- **Put up physical flyers** within a few streets, with a clear photo, last-seen location, and a phone number — not just a QR code, since not everyone will use it
- **Post in local lost-and-found groups** on Facebook and neighbourhood apps, and in any breed-specific or local pet community you're part of
- **Check with your microchip registry** to confirm your details are current, and be ready to give the registry number if a vet clinic or shelter finds them
- **Call local vet clinics and council/animal control**, not just the closest one — a found pet is often taken to whichever clinic is nearest to where they were picked up, not to yours
- **Leave familiar-smelling items outside**, like their bed or an unwashed piece of your clothing, near where they went missing — this genuinely helps some cats find their way back on their own

## What tends not to help

- Chasing a spotted pet, which almost always sends them further away rather than back toward you
- A large, loud group search in the first hours, particularly for cats — noise and unfamiliar people reduce the chance they'll come out of hiding
- Giving up after one search of the immediate area — many recoveries happen days later, within a few houses of where the pet went missing

## If you're the sitter, not the owner

This is the moment permission and contact details, done properly in advance, earn their keep. Call the owner immediately, even if you feel like you should "handle it first" — they may have instincts about hiding spots or behaviour that shortcut the whole search. If a backup decision-maker was named for emergencies, this counts; use them if the owner can't be reached.

## Why this is worth having written down before it happens

The advice above is useless if a sitter is trying to Google it from a driveway at 11pm, phone at 4%, pet nowhere in sight. The two things that actually change outcomes — a recent photo and a confirmed-current microchip number — are exactly the details that belong in a handover before a trip starts, not looked up in the moment.
