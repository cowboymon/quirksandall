# Storms and Fireworks: Briefing a Sitter on Noise Phobia

Separation anxiety gets covered in most handovers now. Noise phobia — the specific, sometimes severe fear response to fireworks, thunder, or other loud sounds — usually doesn't, even though it's extremely common and can look frightening to a sitter who's never seen it before. An estimated 49% of dogs show a significant fear response to firework noise, which makes this closer to "worth planning for" than "unlikely to come up."

## The short answer

If your dog has any history of reacting to loud noises, tell your sitter in advance what it looks like, what helps, and — if relevant — what medication is involved and when to give it. A sitter who knows what's coming can manage it calmly. A sitter who doesn't may not realise what they're looking at, or panic right alongside your dog.

## What noise phobia actually looks like

Signs range from mild to severe, and it's worth describing your dog's specific version rather than a general list:

- Trembling, panting, pacing, or excessive drooling
- Hiding, seeking out a person, or trying to stay glued to your sitter
- Vocalising, whining, or barking
- Digging at doors, walls, or floors, or attempting to escape a room or crate
- In more severe cases, injuring themselves trying to get away from the source of the fear

Some dogs also show anticipatory anxiety — reacting to darkening skies, wind changes, or the "smell of rain" before a storm even starts, which can look confusing to someone who doesn't know that's a pattern for your dog specifically.

## What actually helps

- **A safe space, set up in advance.** An interior room without windows, ideally where your dog already goes when unsettled — a crate covered with a blanket often works well if your dog already uses one
- **Background noise.** A fan, white noise, or the TV at a normal volume can help mask the sound of what's outside
- **Staying calm and present.** Comfort during a genuine fear response is appropriate — sitting with your dog, speaking calmly, and offering physical contact if they want it doesn't reinforce the fear, despite an old myth that it does
- **Avoiding a big walk right as it starts.** If a storm or fireworks are expected, exercise and toilet breaks earlier in the day reduce the chance of being caught outside when it begins
- **Never scolding or punishing the reaction.** It's a genuine panic response, not disobedience, and punishment reliably makes it worse

## Medication

If your dog is on anxiety medication for noise events specifically, this needs to be spelled out clearly for a sitter, not left to guesswork:

- The exact medication, dose, and how far in advance of the expected noise it needs to be given — timing matters, since most anxiety medication needs to be on board before the event starts, not after your dog is already distressed
- Whether it's a standing prescription or something to be used only around known trigger events (fireworks season, a forecast storm)
- What a normal response to the medication looks like, so a sitter isn't alarmed by expected drowsiness or isn't concerned it "isn't working" when it's functioning as intended

## What's manageable versus what's worth a call

**Generally manageable, with the steps above:**
- Panting, pacing, or seeking your sitter out during the event
- Settling within a reasonable time after the noise stops

**Worth a call to you or the vet:**
- Injuring themselves trying to escape a room, crate, or the house
- A reaction that doesn't improve at all once the noise has stopped
- Any sign of the fear escalating rather than settling over repeated events

## Why this belongs in the handover

Fireworks and storms are predictable enough — New Year's Eve, a forecast storm, a local event — that "we might not be here for one" isn't a safe assumption for any multi-day stay. A sitter who knows this is coming can prepare the safe space in advance rather than improvising mid-panic.
