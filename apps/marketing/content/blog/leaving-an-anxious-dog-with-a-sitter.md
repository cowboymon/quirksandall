# Leaving an Anxious Dog With Someone You Trust

If your dog gets visibly distressed when you leave, handing them to a sitter can feel like the hardest part of going anywhere. The good news, according to vets and behaviourists: most dogs with separation anxiety do fine with a sitter, precisely because it's not about you specifically — it's about not being alone.

## The short answer

Prepare your sitter for what to expect, keep your own departure low-key, and make sure they know the difference between manageable anxiety and something to escalate. A well-briefed sitter is often more reassuring for an anxious dog than an empty house would be.

## What to tell your sitter in advance

- What the anxiety actually looks like for your dog — pacing, whining, destructive behaviour, drooling, not eating
- How long it usually takes to settle
- What helps: a particular toy, a specific spot, background noise, a piece of your clothing
- What doesn't help, so they don't waste time or make it worse
- Whether your dog is on any anxiety medication, and the dosing schedule

## Keep departures and arrivals low-key

This applies to you and to your sitter. Vets and trainers consistently recommend keeping comings and goings unremarkable — no big goodbyes, no excited greetings — since a dog picks up on the emotional weight you put on leaving, and a low-key departure and arrival genuinely helps reduce the anxiety response over time.

## What's manageable versus what needs a call

**Generally manageable, with a bit of settling time:**
- Whining or pacing for the first while after you leave
- Not eating immediately, but eating later
- Wanting to be near the sitter rather than alone

**Worth a call to you or the vet:**
- Not eating or drinking at all after an extended period
- Injuring themselves trying to escape a crate or room
- Symptoms that don't improve at all over the course of the stay
- Any sign of the anxiety escalating rather than settling

The professional consensus is clear on one thing in particular: anxious behaviour is a distress response, not disobedience, so a sitter should never scold or punish it — that tends to make things worse, not better.

## Setting your sitter up to actually help

The most reassuring thing for most anxious dogs isn't a special technique — it's a person in the house, sticking to routine, and not treating the anxiety as a crisis every time it shows up. Sitters who know what to expect handle it with far more confidence than sitters working it out in real time.
