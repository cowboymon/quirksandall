# Hot Weather Safety: What a Dog Sitter Needs to Know

A sitter who wouldn't dream of skipping a walk is doing exactly the wrong thing on a genuinely hot day. Heat safety for dogs isn't intuitive if you haven't dealt with it before — the risk isn't just obvious extreme heat, it's specific, checkable things most people don't think to check.

## The short answer

Two things matter more than anything else: whether the pavement is too hot for paws, and whether your dog falls into a higher-risk group. Test the ground, know your dog's risk category, and shift walks to early morning or evening on hot days rather than skipping the check altogether.

## The pavement test

Press the back of your hand flat on the pavement and hold it there for five seconds. If it's too hot for you to keep your hand there, it's too hot for your dog's paws. Ground temperature can reach far higher than the air temperature on a sunny day, and pavement burns aren't always obvious immediately — a sitter unfamiliar with this test might not think to do it at all.

- Grass and dirt stay significantly cooler than asphalt or concrete and are the safer surface on a hot day
- Signs of paw burns include limping, licking or chewing at the feet, and pads that look red, blistered or cracked
- If you suspect burned pads, get your dog off the hot surface immediately and onto grass or somewhere they can be carried

## Which dogs are higher risk

Not every dog needs the same level of caution, and it's worth telling your sitter explicitly which category yours falls into:

- **Flat-faced breeds** (pugs, bulldogs, boxers, and similar) — can't pant as efficiently, so they overheat faster than other dogs even in moderate heat
- **Seniors, puppies, and overweight dogs** — all have reduced ability to regulate temperature
- **Thick or dark-coated dogs** — absorb more heat and take longer to cool down
- **Dogs with existing heart or breathing conditions**

If your dog fits any of these categories, say so plainly rather than assuming a sitter will factor it in — a sitter without your dog's specific risk profile is working from general caution, not your dog's actual limits.

## Timing

Early morning, before the day heats up, or evening once things have cooled off are the safest windows. The middle of the day — roughly late morning through late afternoon — is when both air and ground temperatures peak, and it's worth telling your sitter your actual preferred walk windows for hot weather specifically, not just your normal schedule.

## Signs of overheating

- **Early signs**: heavy panting, excessive drooling, red gums, slowing down or reluctance to continue, seeking shade
- **Serious signs**: stumbling, weakness especially in the back legs, glazed eyes, vomiting, or collapse

Serious signs are a medical emergency. Get your dog into shade or air conditioning immediately, apply cool — not ice-cold — water to their neck, belly and inner thighs, and get to a vet as soon as possible. Early signs are the point to stop the walk and head home, not push on for another block.

## Cars in hot weather

Worth stating explicitly, even though it seems obvious: never leave a dog in a parked car in warm weather, even briefly, even with windows cracked. Car interiors heat up far faster than most people expect, and cracking a window doesn't meaningfully change that.

## What to actually hand over

- Whether your dog is in a higher-risk category, and which one
- Your actual preferred walk windows on hot days, not just your normal schedule
- Whether your dog has had heat issues before
- A reminder on the pavement test, even if it feels obvious — it's the single most-skipped step

## Why this is worth spelling out

Most owners do the pavement test and adjust walk timing automatically, without consciously thinking of it as a "rule" — it's just instinct built from knowing their dog and their neighbourhood. A sitter doesn't have that instinct yet on day one, which is exactly when a hot-weather mistake is most likely.
