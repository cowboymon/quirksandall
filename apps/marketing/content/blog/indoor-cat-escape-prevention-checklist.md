# Indoor Cat Escape Prevention Checklist for Pet Sitters

Most indoor-cat escapes don't happen because a cat runs off. They happen because a door was open a beat too long, and curiosity did the rest. For a sitter who's never met your cat, that risk is higher — they don't yet have the instinct to check before they step through a doorway.

## The short answer

Brief your sitter on entry technique, secure the exits, and give them a plan for if it happens anyway. Most of this is about habits, not equipment.

## Entry and exit technique

- Enter slowly and low-key, rather than walking straight in — a cat that's been waiting by the door has a head start on a fast, casual entry
- Use a body block: open the door only as far as needed and step through immediately, rather than swinging it wide
- Come in the same way you or a regular household member usually would, if possible — an unfamiliar entry point can unsettle a cat and increase door-dashing
- Don't linger in doorways — that's the highest-risk moment of any visit

## Securing the house

- Confirm all windows and screens are secure before the sitter's first visit, not after
- Flag any doors that don't latch properly or screens that are less secure than they look
- Note any rooms with balcony or window access that need to stay closed
- Mention if your cat is a known "door dasher" so the sitter's on alert from visit one, not after the first close call

## What to leave with your sitter

- A recent photo, for anything that needs sharing quickly
- Your cat's microchip status and registration details
- Confirmation of whether they're strictly indoor, and why (some indoor cats have outdoor access elsewhere and this can confuse a new sitter)

## If it happens anyway

Professional pet sitters are trained to expect this and stay calm rather than chase — chasing tends to send a startled cat further, not back inside. The general guidance: stay calm, don't corner or chase, and try food or a familiar sound to encourage them back rather than pursuit. Have your sitter contact you immediately either way, so you can help think through likely hiding spots nearby.

## Why this is worth writing down, not just saying

A rushed verbal warning at the door — "oh, and don't let her out" — is easy to forget under pressure the first time it actually matters. Having it written down, checked before the first visit rather than remembered mid-visit, is what actually prevents the problem instead of just reacting to it.
