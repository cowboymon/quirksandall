# Briefing a Sitter for a Multi-Pet Household

One pet is a handover. Two or more is a system — feeding order, who's allowed near whom, whose medication looks identical to whose. Most pet-care advice is written for a single animal, which means owners with a cat and a dog, or three of one species, end up guessing at what to add rather than following anything written for their situation.

## The short answer

Cover each pet individually first, the same way you would if they were the only one — then add a separate layer for how they interact with each other, because that's the part single-pet advice never has to mention. Feeding order, supervision needs, and who gets walked or let out first are usually the details that actually determine whether a multi-pet stay goes smoothly.

## Start with individual profiles, not a combined list

It's tempting to write one shared page — "they eat at 6pm, walk at 7am" — but pets in the same house rarely have identical needs. Give each pet their own section covering the basics: food, routine, medical details, quirks. A sitter juggling two or three animals needs to be able to find "the cat's information" or "the older dog's medication" without wading through a merged list built around whichever pet you thought of first.

## Then cover how they interact

This is the part that's genuinely specific to multi-pet households and easy to leave out:

- **Feeding order and separation.** Does one pet eat faster and try to finish the other's bowl? Do they need to be fed in separate rooms, or is free feeding fine?
- **Supervision needs.** Are they fine unsupervised together, or does one need to be crated, separated, or watched during specific moments — mealtimes, new visitors, high-energy play?
- **Whose turn it is.** If both dogs get walked, do they go together or separately, and does one dog change how the other behaves on lead?
- **Medication mix-ups.** If more than one pet is on medication, especially similar-looking tablets, say clearly which pet gets which — this is one of the easier things to get wrong under pressure, and one of the more serious ones.
- **The established pecking order.** Which pet gets fed first, greeted first, or let outside first, if that matters to keeping the peace. Owners often don't realise how much of this is instinctive until a stranger doesn't know it.

## Cats and dogs together

For mixed households specifically:

- Note whether the cat has an escape route from the dog (a baby gate, a high shelf, a room the dog can't access) and make sure the sitter knows to keep it clear
- Flag whether the dog is reliable around the cat unsupervised or needs active management, particularly around food and toys
- Mention if the cat's hiding spots are different when the dog's sitter is also in the house, since a new person plus an excitable dog is a bigger disruption than either alone

## What tends to go wrong without this

A sitter without the interaction layer will treat multiple pets as separate, unrelated tasks — feed pet A, feed pet B, walk pet C — and miss the parts that only matter because they're together. That's how a food-guarding issue turns into a vet visit, or how a cat ends up cornered because nobody mentioned which room was meant to stay off-limits to the dog.

## The debrief matters more here too

With multiple pets, more can quietly shift between stays — one pet's routine changes, a new pecking order develops, a previously fine pairing starts needing supervision. A quick check-in with your sitter after the stay (see our piece on the post-trip debrief) is worth doing for every multi-pet household, since there's simply more that could have changed than with a single pet.
