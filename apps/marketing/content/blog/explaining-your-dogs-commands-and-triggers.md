# How to Explain Your Dog's Commands, Triggers and Walking Routine

You know exactly what your dog needs on a walk without thinking about it. A sitter doesn't have that instinct yet — they only have what you tell them, which means the gap between "obvious to you" and "written down clearly" is exactly where a walk goes sideways.

## Commands: say the real ones, not the textbook ones

Every dog has their own version of "sit," "stay," and "come" — the specific word, tone, or hand signal that actually gets a response, which isn't always the standard one. If your dog only listens to "off" and not "down," say that explicitly. A sitter using the technically-correct command and getting nothing isn't a training problem — it's a handover gap.

Also worth including:
- Which commands are solid versus still a work in progress
- Any command that means something different to your dog than it would to most ("stay" that means five seconds, not five minutes)
- Physical cues or hand signals, if your dog responds better to those than words

## Triggers: specific, not general

"He's fine with most things" isn't useful. What a sitter needs is the specific stuff:
- Other dogs — all of them, or a particular type or size
- Specific sounds — bin trucks, skateboards, other dogs barking
- People in certain clothing — hats, high-vis, uniforms
- Situations — being approached from behind, tight spaces, off-lead dogs approaching

Say what the reaction actually looks like too — a bark that's all noise and no bite is a very different heads-up to a sitter than one that means "create distance now."

## Walking routine: the version that's actually true

- Real length and frequency, not the aspirational version
- On or off lead, and specifically where off-lead is safe
- The route you actually take, and anywhere to avoid
- Recall reliability — honestly, including the specific situations where it drops off (a dog with perfect recall in the backyard and none near other dogs is common, and worth flagging exactly like that)

## Why the honest version matters more than the tidy version

It's tempting to smooth over the parts that feel like admitting your dog isn't perfectly behaved. That's exactly the information a sitter needs most — not to judge your dog, but to know what to actually expect and adjust for, rather than finding out mid-walk.
