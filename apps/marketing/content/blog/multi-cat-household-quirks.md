# Multi-Cat Households: The Quirks a Single-Cat Handover Misses

A handover written for one cat, then duplicated for a second, misses the entire point of what makes multi-cat households different. Cats sharing a home aren't just two separate pets who happen to live together — there's a whole layer of resource management and social dynamics invisible to anyone who's only ever had one cat, and it's exactly the layer a sitter needs to know about.

## The short answer

The single biggest thing to get right in a multi-cat household is resource count and placement — litter boxes, food, and water — because most conflict between cats comes down to competition over these, not personality clashes. Get the resources right and a lot of the rest resolves itself.

## The litter box rule that changes everything

Vets and behaviourists consistently recommend the "n+1" rule: one litter box per cat, plus one extra. Two cats need three boxes, not two. This isn't arbitrary — cats are naturally territorial about elimination, and even cats who get along well generally still want their own space for this specifically.

- **Boxes need to be spread out, not clustered.** Two boxes side by side function as one box in a cat's perception — put them in separate rooms or at least separate corners
- **One per floor, if your home has more than one level** — a set of stairs can function as a real barrier, especially for an older or less confident cat
- **Watch for quiet guarding.** A more confident cat can block a shyer one from a box just by loitering nearby, without any obvious aggression — this often goes unnoticed by owners and is worth specifically flagging to a sitter as something to watch for
- **A cat suddenly avoiding the litter box** — especially with straining, crying, or blood — is a potential medical emergency, not just a behavioural issue, and shouldn't be assumed to be resource competition without ruling that out

## Feeding

- **Separate stations, not one shared bowl** — even bonded cats often prefer not to eat side by side, and a faster eater will otherwise finish a slower cat's portion
- **Whether any cat needs portion control or a special diet**, and how you currently prevent the others from getting into it
- **If tension shows up specifically at mealtimes**, say so — some households need cats fed in different rooms entirely to avoid guarding or conflict

## The quiet dynamics worth naming

This is the part that's genuinely invisible to someone meeting your cats for the first time:

- **The established pecking order**, if there is one — who eats first, who gets greeted first, who tends to get out of the way when the other approaches
- **Which pairing needs supervision, if any** — not every multi-cat household has tension, but if yours does, say specifically when and where it shows up
- **A cat who's recently changed behaviour** — increased hiding, new spraying, avoiding a housemate — since this is often the first visible sign of a resource or health issue rather than "just personality"

## What tends to go wrong without this

A sitter treating multiple cats as separate, identical tasks — feed cat A, feed cat B, scoop the box — will miss exactly the parts that only matter because they're sharing a space. That's how one cat quietly stops using the litter box because a housemate's been blocking it, weeks before anyone notices the mess.

## Why this deserves its own section, not a duplicated profile

Two complete, separate cat profiles tell a sitter about each cat individually but nothing about how they relate — which is often the actual determinant of whether a stay goes smoothly. The household-level picture is a different kind of information to per-cat details, and it needs its own place, not to be inferred.

Quirks & All keeps each cat's profile complete and separate, with the household dynamics — resource layout, pecking order, anything to watch — sitting alongside them, so a sitter looking after multiple cats sees the whole picture, not three unrelated to-do lists.
